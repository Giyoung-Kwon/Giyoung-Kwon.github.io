Working Papers

Addiction Runs: Rank Competition and Strategic Complementarities in Prescription Stimulant Usage
(with Chanwool Kim and Younggeun Yoo)
Presentations: Workshop in Behavioral and Experimental Health Economics (2026, Scheduled)

Abstract

We study the rise of prescription stimulant use for academic performance using a model with relative-performance externalities. Students compete for rank, where taking a stimulant improves effective performance but entails costs. Because payoffs from academic competition depend on others' performance, students' stimulant usage exhibits strategic complementarities: as more individuals use stimulants, others face stronger incentives to follow in order to avoid falling behind. We identify the model using prescription trends in the U.S. and our own survey evidence on students' incentives, beliefs, and perceived costs. The estimated strategic complementarities are large enough to generate multiple equilibria, including one with low use and another with very high use, in which around 90 percent of students take stimulants. We use the estimated model to evaluate policies that (i) reduce the salience of relative evaluation, (ii) limit the competitive advantage conferred by stimulants, particularly through academic accommodations, (iii) separate academic competition between stimulant users and non-users (i.e., separate curves), and (iv) tighten prescribing standards to reduce non-medical use. Our estimates show that separate curves yield rankings that most closely reflect underlying ability and are the policy that least discourages true ADHD patients from taking prescribed stimulants.

Gambling with Expensive Financial Lotteries
(with Spencer Yongwook Kwon, Jaesang Lee, Seung Hyeong Lee, and Haesung Kim)

Abstract

Retail investors use options as financial lotteries, gambling on a small chance of large, immediate gains. Using the universe of account-level transactions in KOSPI 200 options during a period when Korea hosted one of the world’s largest options markets, we show that small retail investors disproportionately buy very short-dated, lottery-like options while financial intermediaries supply these contracts and hedge the resulting exposures. Unlike conventional lottery tickets, these financial lotteries are endogenously expensive: their prices reflect compensation for the volatility risk borne by the intermediaries that provide them. As a consequence, we find that small retail investors incur substantial aggregate trading losses. Delta-hedged losses on their option positions amount to about 90% of their aggregate trading losses, consistent with the cost of purchasing volatility. Compensation for volatility risk accounts for a substantial share of this cost, but losses unexplained by risk exposure still constitute approximately one-third of the remaining losses and are especially large when implied volatility rises, expiration is near, and options are out of the money. Our findings suggest that lottery-like payoffs in financial markets can carry substantial costs even in the absence of abnormal intermediary profits, as suppliers require compensation for bearing volatility risk.

The Market for Convexity: Retail Option Demand and the Allocation of Risk
(with Jingoo Kwon, Seung Hyeong Lee, Seongkyu Gilbert Park, Younggeun Yoo, and Haesung Kim)
Presentations: Conference on Asia-Pacific Financial Markets (2026, Scheduled)

Abstract

We examine retail demand for convexity and the allocation and pricing of option risk in a market with substantial retail participation. Using the universe of account-level KOSPI 200 index option transactions from 2010 to 2014, when Korea hosted one of the world’s largest options markets, we show that small retail investors buy to open nearly 97% of the contracts. Small retail investors consistently buy both calls and puts, even as their directional exposures change. Buy-opened positions lose on average for both retail and institutional investors other than market makers, whereas sell-opened positions earn profits, consistent with compensation for bearing variance risk. Market makers trade in high volumes relative to their overnight positions, while large retail investors hold the largest share of overnight sell positions. Large retail investors bear more unhedged crash risk than institutional writers, which incur substantial losses during the market decline due to the U.S. credit rating downgrade. The investor groups offsetting demand by small retail investors vary with the trading horizon, and large retail investors’ daily net sales primarily offsetting foreign institutional net purchases. Implied volatility is higher when large retail investors hold smaller net sell positions, consistent with shifts in their risk-bearing capacity. Our findings link persistent retail demand for leveraged option exposure to the allocation and pricing of option risk across investors with distinct portfolio exposures and downside risks.
